Compliance · Process design

Regulatory onboarding SOP for a messaging platform

Problem

Client onboarding ran on carrier-registration rules that lived in two people’s heads. The framework named the phases that block a go-live, but nothing told anyone how to execute them — in a regime where a rejected submission costs a fee per attempt and a wrong one costs the account.

Built

A twelve-section onboarding and provisioning lifecycle with two sendable annexes: qualification, the intake package, US A2P 10DLC compliance rules, the portal steps for brand and campaign registration in order, pilot and verification, go-live and handover, carrier redundancy and failover, and a definition of done. Writing it surfaced two live defects nobody had recorded — accounts provisioned under the parent rather than per client cannot be moved afterwards and quietly break tenant segregation, and a cloned campaign carries the previous client’s consent evidence and live checkout URL into the next submission. Both are hard warnings in the procedure now. Since then the work moved from writing the rules to applying them: pre-submission audits of client websites, carrier-registration packages, a client-requirements standard, and a double-opt-in standard adopted after one campaign was sent back twice. One site audit caught a checkout bug where a changed answer kept its old value and the funnel told a visitor they could lose 819 lbs — flagged separately so it would not read as a registration blocker.

How I work

The habits that make the systems hold up

Anyone can ship a workflow. The reason this operation still runs unattended is a set of rules I apply to my own work — most of them learned the expensive way, and each one written down so it is never learned twice.

01Measure it; don’t assume it

Numbers get date-stamped and re-measured before they are quoted. When five documents disagreed on a workflow count, the fix was not to pick one — it was to define the single place the measurement lives and record when it was taken. Every claim on this page came from a measurement, not a memory.

02A success report is not evidence

“It ran” and “it worked” are different claims. Writes are verified by reading back through a path independent of the write. Deploys are verified inside the running container, not from the deploy script’s own output. Several things that reported success had done nothing at all.

03An alarm that never clears is not an alarm

A permanently-raised alert is a disabled one. Before hardening a check, I confirm its output channel still carries information — otherwise the work looks like progress and changes nothing. The fix is usually to scope the signal, not to grind through the noise it produced.

04Enforce at the choke point, not in a report

A rule that lives in a document is advice. The media eligibility engine only started working the day the picker read it — one join, and four separate consumers inherited the verdict. Put the check where the decision is actually made.

05Degrade loudly, never silently

Systems that fail quietly fail three times before anyone notices. Staleness is signalled from something that cannot be faked by a failed run, surfaced on the page it affects, and made non-dismissible. Restrictions are additive, so a stale check under-restricts — it can never permit more than the old behaviour.

06Gates before sends, humans on the trigger

Nothing customer-facing or people-facing auto-sends. Drafts are drafts. Quoted evidence is machine-verified against its source, output is schema-validated, and a person signs off. Automation removes the work, not the accountability.

07Write it down where the next person reads

Durable facts get promoted out of a live working file into permanent memory on a defined rule. Rejected paths are recorded alongside the chosen one, so nobody re-litigates a settled decision. Fifteen AI agents and every new hire read the same source of truth.

08Say plainly what is not done

Every status carries what landed, what is staged behind a gate, and what is still open — with the reason. A thing that is 2 of 5 steps built gets described as 2 of 5. Optimistic reporting is the most expensive habit in operations.

About

The person behind the systems

I sit between the systems and the people — building the infrastructure that makes distributed teams visible, accountable and scalable, then running the team inside it.

I started on the call center floor and worked up through team leading, training and sales management to running a 300-seat program. Then I taught myself to build the tools I kept wishing existed. Today that means a production stack I administer, an automation layer of several hundred workflows, an internal CRM the whole floor works in, a data warehouse behind every number I report, and a documented knowledge base that lets both people and AI agents pick the work up without me in the room. I run on measurement, clean process, and the belief that good systems are what make good managers.

The work comes in two shapes, and I like both. One is a 24/7 operation where the job is infrastructure and people at scale. The other is an early company with a real product and almost nothing written down, where the job is turning what the founders know into procedures, documents and roles a team can actually run.

If you have an operation where the numbers are guesses, the tooling is spreadsheets, and the knowledge lives in three people’s heads — that is exactly the shape of problem I take on.
